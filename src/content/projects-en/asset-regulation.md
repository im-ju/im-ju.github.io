## Results

I rewrote an asset management regulation that had gone unchanged for more than two years, got it approved by the CEO, and then used its new standard to pick the equipment actually due for replacement.

- **15 changes applied.** The most important of the problems found from three angles.
- **3 new clauses and 1 new form.** Clauses on loss and theft, data security, and what happens when the regulation is breached, plus a table of asset categories and how many years each is expected to last.
- **17 people recommended for replacement.** I applied the new obsolescence standard to the asset register and sorted them by priority.

## The problem

The asset management regulation was still the November 2023 version. Since then the company and its assets had grown, and situations the regulation did not cover kept coming up in day-to-day work.

- The procedure for returning equipment when someone leaves was not laid out in steps. The checks done in practice needed to be written into the regulation.
- It was unclear how much someone should pay, and how, when an asset was lost or damaged. Deducting it from pay unilaterally could have been a legal problem.
- There was no standard for "obsolete equipment", so every replacement request was judged case by case.
- The regulation covered rental assets and software licences, but the company had no rental assets, and licences were handled by information security. The regulation did not match what was actually being managed.

## What I did

**I read the regulation from three angles.** One person reading it in one pass sees only the problems they are used to. A practitioner sees gaps in procedure, a lawyer sees who is liable, a security person sees the data. So I read the same regulation three separate times:

- **Legal and tax**: wage deductions under the Labor Standards Act, useful life under the Corporate Tax Act (how many years a piece of equipment is treated as being in use), and tax treatment when assets are disposed of.
- **Information security**: how data is erased when someone leaves, when equipment is returned and when it is disposed of, and laptop security settings.
- **Structure and practice**: undefined terms, gaps in procedure, and places where forms and clauses disagreed.

I sorted what came out by priority and put the 15 most important items into this revision.

**I defined the words the regulation needed to point at.** Without definitions, people read the clauses differently. I added definitions for the asset register (the document recording what assets the company holds), available stock (assets that have been recovered, wiped and checked, and can be issued again), obsolescence (four years after purchase, based on the Enforcement Rules of the Corporate Tax Act) and the asset return form (a form recording the returned items, the reason, and confirmation that data was erased). The obsolescence standard in particular moved replacement decisions from personal discretion to a rule.

**I broke procedures into steps.** Issuing equipment was a single line: "an acceptance form must be obtained." I split it into four steps: request, stock check, acceptance form (recording the condition on handover) and an entry in the asset register. I also wrote in the principle that new hires receive their equipment by their start date. For returns, the deadline (the last working day) and three security steps (erasing data, removing system access, recording it on the return form) are now in the clauses.

**I closed off the legal risk in compensation.** Compensation now varies in tiers by degree of fault, and the regulation states that it is never deducted unilaterally from wages or severance pay; it can only be claimed through a separate compensation agreement. This clause puts legal soundness ahead of convenience.

**I matched the forms to the clauses.** If only the clauses change, the paper people actually use stays the same, and nothing really changes. I added asset-category checkboxes and a field for the condition on handover to the acceptance form, and confirmation boxes for data erasure and account removal (signed by the security officer) to the return form.

**I did not edit the regulation documents by hand.** A regulation needs consistent indentation and numbering at every level, from article down to paragraph and item, and hand edits always drift. It is also a document that gets regenerated several times as review comments come in. So I had a program produce both the regulation text and the revision history from the same source. The revised text and its history cannot contradict each other, and the reason for each change sits right next to the source.

**I made a real decision with the new regulation.** I applied the obsolescence standard from the revision, together with leadership's criteria (job role, years the equipment had been in use, memory spec), to the asset register and picked 17 people for laptop replacement. Instead of one long ranking, I split them into top priority and next priority, so that whatever the budget turned out to be, it could be cut from the top down. I also listed the equipment's age and how long its current user had had it, separately: two four-year-old laptops are not the same if one person has used one from the start and the other was handed over partway through. Applying the standard to real data also revealed entries where the asset register did not match reality. I reported those too and carried them over to the next revision.

## What I chose not to do

**I did not try to fix everything at once.** Only the 15 most important items went into this revision; the rest stayed in the document as "items to review in the next revision". Trying to fix everything at once would have delayed the revision itself.

**I did not keep clauses that did not match reality.** The clause on rental assets, which the company did not have, and the clause on software licences, which belonged to another area, were brought in line with what the regulation actually manages.

**I did not let the regulation stop at paper.** Without a standard, every decision is a judgement call, and without making a decision you cannot tell whether the standard fits reality. So I used the approved standard right away to pick the replacements.
